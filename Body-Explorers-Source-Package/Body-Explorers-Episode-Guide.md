# Body Explorers
## A 20-episode first season

**Audience:** children aged 6–9. **Language:** English. **Length:** approximately five minutes per episode. **Format:** original 3D animated adventures with a warm narrator, readable captions, and a short interactive recap.

**The cast:** Pip is a curious miniature robot who asks the questions children might ask. Ruby is a friendly red blood cell and the recurring guide to the bloodstream. Maya is the child whose everyday adventures launch the mysteries. Additional organ and cell characters appear only where they help the story. Faces, voices, glowing molecules, and miniature explorers are explicitly fictional teaching devices.

**Pilot:** The Great Oxygen Delivery — completed as a 5:00, 1280 × 720, 24 fps MP4 with English narration, original quiet instrumental music, animated 3D models, and captions.

## Repeatable five-minute structure

- **0:00–0:30:** An everyday mystery and the mission.
- **0:30–1:30:** Meet the system and explain essential vocabulary through action.
- **1:30–3:30:** Follow a process, with a clear beginning, middle, and result.
- **3:30–4:30:** Resolve the mystery and connect it to the child's everyday experience.
- **4:30–5:00:** Two or three recall questions, an encouraging recap, and the next adventure.

Use one main question per episode. Keep scary imagery, medical procedures, and graphic anatomy out of the visual language. Let children think before revealing quiz answers. Avoid describing illnesses or body differences as personal failures.

## Pilot narration and scene plan

### 00:00 — The Great Oxygen Delivery

A big race is about to begin. But deep inside Maya's body, a tiny delivery team is already on the move! Meet Pip, a curious little explorer, and Ruby, a red blood cell with a very important job.

On-screen teaching label: A BODY EXPLORERS ADVENTURE

### 00:15 — A Very Important Mission

Hello, explorers! Ruby says. Maya's muscles will need more oxygen as she runs. Pip checks Ruby's empty delivery spots. We'd better fill up! Their first stop is the lungs. Come along!

On-screen teaching label: Meet Ruby • Red blood cell

### 00:30 — Small World. Big Adventure.

Real blood cells don't have eyes, voices, or little explorers riding beside them. Oxygen is too small to see like these sparkles. Our cartoon makes an invisible journey easier to imagine.

On-screen teaching label: Cartoon characters • Real science

### 00:45 — Take A Breath

Maya breathes in. Air enters through her nose or mouth, travels down her windpipe, and follows branching airways into her lungs. Pip watches the branches divide. It's an upside-down tree of tunnels!

On-screen teaching label: Air travels into the lungs

### 01:00 — The Tiny Air-Sac Garden

The smallest branches end in tiny air sacs called alveoli. Around them are very small blood vessels. Air and blood come close together here, but thin walls keep them separated.

On-screen teaching label: Alveoli = tiny air sacs

### 01:15 — Oxygen Crosses Over

Oxygen moves across those thin walls into the blood. It doesn't need a door or a robot. This happens naturally. Pip follows our glowing oxygen dots from the air sac toward Ruby.

On-screen teaching label: From air sacs → into blood

### 01:30 — Ruby Loads Up

Inside red blood cells, a protein called hemoglobin can hold oxygen. Think of it as a helper that picks oxygen up and later lets it go. Ruby is ready! Her oxygen cargo will help Maya's muscles keep working.

On-screen teaching label: Hemoglobin holds oxygen

### 01:45 — Next Stop: The Heart

Whoosh! Blood carries Ruby away from the lungs and back to the left side of the heart. Pip hears a steady beat ahead. Is that a drum? It's a pump, says Ruby. And it's about to give us a push!

On-screen teaching label: Lungs → left side of the heart

### 02:00 — The Amazing Heart Pump

The heart is a strong muscle with four chambers. It squeezes to push blood onward, then relaxes to fill again. One-way valves help control the direction. Our model shows a simplified heart.

On-screen teaching label: A muscle that pumps blood

### 02:15 — Two Sides. One Team.

The right side of the heart sends blood to the lungs. The left side sends blood to the rest of the body. Ruby has just returned from the lungs, so the left side now pumps her toward Maya's leg muscles.

On-screen teaching label: Right → lungs | Left → body

### 02:30 — Follow The Blood Vessels

Ruby rides through an artery, a vessel carrying blood away from the heart. It branches into smaller vessels. Other red blood cells travel nearby. Nobody needs a steering wheel. The flowing blood carries them!

On-screen teaching label: Arteries carry blood away from the heart

### 02:45 — The Narrowest Streets

Ruby reaches tiny vessels called capillaries, where blood comes close to body cells. Red blood cells are flexible, helping them fit through narrow spaces. Ruby gently changes shape. Special delivery for the muscles!

On-screen teaching label: Capillaries = tiny blood vessels

### 03:00 — Delivery Complete!

Oxygen leaves the blood and moves into muscle tissue. Pip cheers as Ruby's cargo arrives. Remember, oxygen doesn't really glow. We're lighting it up so you can follow its path.

On-screen teaching label: Oxygen moves from blood into tissues

### 03:15 — How Cells Get Energy

Muscle cells use oxygen to help release usable energy from nutrients in food. Oxygen isn't food or energy by itself. The supplies work together, helping Maya's muscles contract and move her legs.

On-screen teaching label: Nutrients + oxygen help cells release energy

### 03:30 — Maya Starts Running

Maya takes off! Her muscles contract and relax. Her breathing and heartbeat usually speed up to help meet her muscles' needs. Ruby isn't alone. Huge numbers of blood cells keep deliveries moving.

On-screen teaching label: Working muscles need more energy

### 03:45 — There'S A Return Trip!

Making usable energy also produces carbon dioxide, a waste gas. It enters the blood. Much of it travels in a changed form in blood's liquid part. Time to return to the lungs!

On-screen teaching label: Carbon dioxide travels back in blood

### 04:00 — Back Through The Heart

Veins carry blood back to the right side of the heart, which pumps it to the lungs. Ruby has less oxygen, but she's still red! Blood inside your body is never blue.

On-screen teaching label: Body → right heart → lungs

### 04:15 — Breathe Out. Start Again.

At the air sacs, carbon dioxide moves from blood into the lungs. Maya breathes it out. Fresh oxygen enters the blood. Ruby loads up again. The loop keeps going, even while you're asleep.

On-screen teaching label: Carbon dioxide leaves in exhaled air

### 04:30 — Your Turn, Explorer!

Explorer challenge! Which brings air close to blood: lungs or stomach? The lungs! Which pumps blood: heart or bone? The heart! Who helps carry oxygen? Red blood cells, using hemoglobin. Great work!

On-screen teaching label: Point to the answer before Pip does

### 04:45 — Mission Accomplished

Maya finishes, and Ruby starts another trip. Lungs exchange gases. The heart pumps blood. Blood delivers oxygen. What an amazing team! Next time, Pip follows a sandwich to discover what happens after you swallow.

On-screen teaching label: Next adventure: The Sandwich Journey
